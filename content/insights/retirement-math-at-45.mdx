This is a composite of a family we see often. Two incomes, one teenager, a flat in Powai with eight years left on the loan. The primary earner is 45 and wants to stop working at 60.

## Step 1: What does life cost at 60?

Household spending today, excluding EMI and school fees: ₹1.2 lakh a month.

At 6% inflation for 15 years, that becomes ₹2.88 lakh a month. Roughly ₹34.5 lakh a year. That is the number the plan has to deliver, every year, rising with inflation.

## Step 2: How big does the corpus need to be?

We plan for 25 years after retirement, to age 85. We assume the corpus earns 7% in retirement, mostly in debt and hybrid funds, and that expenses keep rising at 6%.

That 1% real return means the corpus needs to be about 21 times the first year's spending.

**Corpus needed at 60: about ₹7.2 crore.**

## Step 3: What is already working?

This family has ₹1.1 crore across EPF, PPF and mutual funds. Assume 10% a year for 15 years. That grows to about ₹4.6 crore.

**Gap: ₹2.6 crore.**

## Step 4: The monthly number

At an assumed 11% from an equity-heavy portfolio, a monthly SIP of about **₹57,000 for 15 years** closes the gap.

That looked impossible to them. So we split it:

- ₹38,000 starts now, stepped up 5% every year with increments.
- ₹22,000 more starts in year 9, when the home loan EMI of ₹48,000 ends, also stepped up 5% a year.

Together, the two tranches land at about the same ₹2.6 crore.

The second tranche costs nothing they feel today. It is money that is already leaving their account every month, simply redirected.

## What changes the answer most

We tested each assumption. Retiring at 58 instead of 60 adds about ₹1.4 crore to the gap. Spending ₹20,000 less a month in retirement removes about ₹1.2 crore. A 1% lower return on the SIP adds about ₹8,000 a month.

The biggest lever was not returns. It was the spending number. Which is why our first meeting is always about the budget, not the funds.
